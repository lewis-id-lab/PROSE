Abstract

Background. Variants in PTX3, CLEC7A, TLR4 and CD209 are often reported to be associated with invasive aspergillosis, and adding genotype to clinical risk scores has been proposed clinical management. Whether incorporation of genetic risk factors would change clinical decisions has not been tested.

Methods. In a pre-specified simulation study, we added host genotype to an established 60-day clinical risk score for proven or probable invasive mould disease (IMD) in patients with hematological malignancies. The original clinical risk score was based on 4,078 haematology admissions in 1,945 patients (Bologna, 2007–2016; 133 events). Effects for four haplotype terms were deverived from a systematic review (60 studies included) and pooled with Bayesian random-effects models. The effects of genetic mutations were added to the clinical logit with an intercept for each clinical profile, so that the clinical score stayed calibrated and any gain reflected the genetic information. Net-benefit gains were computed exactly for 2,000 posterior draws. The primary endpoint was the gain in identifying patients at a 5% risk threshold beyong the clinical risk score alone. It was judged against a pre-specified minimum clinically important difference (MCID) of 20% more true cases at no extra false positives.

Results. Pooled odds ratios per copy of the risk haplotype ranged from 0.84 (CLEC7A Y238X) to 1.80 (CD209). With the true effects in the score, the four-term panel raised net benefit at 5% by a median of +0.00056 (90% range 0.00015 to 0.00127). That is 1.1 extra true cases per 1,000 admissions, against 4.6 required. At most 0.6% of draws reached the MCID, although the area under the ROC curve rose from 0.861 to 0.873 and the continuous net reclassification improvement was 34%. A second CLEC7A haplotype doubled the gain to +0.00115, about a quarter of the MCID. Neutropenic admissions (80% of events) supplied about four-fifths of the gain. No panel of up to 20 independent loci with per-allele odds ratios of 1.5 or less reached the MCID, and a scored variant correlated 0.7 with the causal one kept under half of the gain. If only 80% of cases were genotype-sensitive, reaching the MCID took about twice as many loci (for example, 19 to 20 rather than 10 at an odds ratio of 2.0), and at 60% or less no architecture in the grid reached it. A single global intercept, used in an earlier version of the simulation, gave gains 5% to 6% larger at 5%, and comparison with the correctly marginalised clinical risk showed that this was not a correction of miscalibration.

Conclusions. On the published evidence, genotype at these genes is unlikely to change decisions at the 5% threshold in haematological inpatients. Doing so would need much larger effects, or many more loci, than candidate-gene studies report. A genotyped, neutropenia-stratified cohort of this size could test whether genetic effects depend on the inflammatory state of the host.

Introduction

Invasive mould disease, mostly invasive aspergillosis, remains a major cause of morbidity and death in patients with haematological malignancies (1). Decisions about antifungal prophylaxis, diagnostic work-up and pre-emptive treatment depends on the estiamted risk of an indiviudal patient for developing invasive mold disease (IMD). Clinical risk scores have been proposed to facilitate individualised risk estaimtes for patients. Stanzani and colleagues derived a score from seven routinely recorded host factors in 4,127 admissions to the haematology unit in Bologna (2). It reached an aROC of 0.85 for proven or probable IMD within 60 days and differentiated high-risk populations at a predicted probability of 5%.

Host genetics might add information that clinical factors do not capture. Candidate-gene studies have reported associations between invasive aspergillosis and variants in genes of innate antifungal immunity: the long pentraxin PTX3, the β-glucan receptor dectin-1 (CLEC7A), Toll-like receptor 4 and DC-SIGN (CD209) (3–8). After allogeneic transplantation, the reconstituted innate immune system is donor-derived, which has led to proposals to use donor genotype to stratify transplant recipients (3). These findings have prompted proposals for genetically augmented risk scores.

Two problems make such proposals difficult to clinically implement. First, the evidence on genetic risk factors for IMD is still relatively small and heterogeneous. Linked variants in the same gene are often reported as separate findings, and adding them to a model as independent terms counts the same signal more than once. Second, gains in discrimination or reclassification do not show whether anyone would be managed differently. Decision-curve analysis is one approach that can be used to address this issue, as it counts true and false positives at a clinically chosen risk threshold, weighted by the harm-to-benefit ratio that the threshold implies (9).

We asked how large, how common, and how independent host genetic effects would need to be to change clinical decisions about IMD in patients treated for haematological malignancies, and whether the published evidence supports this thresholds. Our objectives were to (i) estimate the clinical baseline in the derivation cohort and compare it with the published equation; (ii) quantify the decision value of the best-evidenced variants, using effects pooled from the literature; (iii) test whether genetic effects are larger in neutropenic admissions, as the inflammatory-levelling hypothesis predicts if genotype matters most when the host has no strong inflammatory response;and (iv) identify the genetic architectures- i.e. how many risk variants there are, how strong each one is (odds ratio per allele), and how common each one is (risk-allele frequency)- that would produce a important changes in risk assessment beyond clinical factors is the risk score.

Methods

Design and pre-specification

This is a simulation study that combines individual admission-level clinical data with a meta-analysis of the published genetic evidence. The design is summarised below in the ADEMP structure for simulation studies (10). Design decisions were recorded before any genetic result existed and locked. To add genetic risk to the simulated patients, the original plan adjusted everyone’s risk by the same amount, so that the overall rate of IMD stayed at the observed 3.26%. On review, we found that this left the clinical score slightly wrong for some groups of patients with the same clinical risk factors: it under- or over-estimated their risk in the simulated population. Part of any gain from adding genotype could therefore have come from correcting that error, rather than from the genetic information itself. We therefore changed the primary analysis so that risk was adjusted separately within each group of patients with the same clinical risk factors. The clinical score then stays accurate for every group, and any gain can come only from genotype. The original method was kept as a sensitivity analysis. The change lowered the primary gains slightly (by 5% to 6% at the 5% threshold) and did not alter any conclusion, but because it was made after the earlier results had been seen, it was not blind to them. All secondary analyses were rerun under the new method, with the same random seeds and the same draws of the genetic effects.

Clinical cohort and baseline model

The clinical data are the admission-level derivation data of the Stanzani score, from the BOSCORE registry of the haematology unit in Bologna (admissions 2007–2016). The outcome is proven or probable IMD by EORTC criteria within 60 days of admission. The seven predictors are:

  • prior mould disease;
  • high-dose corticosteroids;
  • uncontrolled malignancy;
  • high-risk chemotherapy;
  • prolonged neutropenia;
  • severe lymphopenia;
  • cytomegalovirus infection.

The published analysis file has 4,127 rows. These are exactly the rows of the parent registry extract that are complete on the seven items. Forty-nine admission numbers appeared twice, each time as an event row plus a non-event row with the same patient and predictor profile. We confirmed these pairs as a recording artefact and removed the duplicate row. The base cohort is therefore 4,078 unique admissions in 1,945 patients, with 133 events (3.26%). Only counts and pseudonymised fields were used, and no patient-level data are in the repository (see Ethics and Data availability).

The baseline is a maximum-likelihood logistic regression of the outcome on the seven predictors in the base cohort. Its uncertainty was handled as follows:

  • Standard errors are clustered by patient.
  • The bootstrap resamples patients and repeats the refit in each of 2,000 resamples.
  • Optimism was estimated by Harrell’s bootstrap method (11). It is reported, not subtracted.

The baseline is held fixed from here on. The joint distribution of clinical risk is the observed distribution of the 128 possible predictor profiles, 75 of which occur. Three alternatives were kept as sensitivity analyses:

  • the published equation;
  • the 4,127-row cohort;
  • a Gaussian-copula reconstruction of the predictor correlation, fitted to the published marginal counts. It was carried as an ensemble of 150 near-optimal correlation matrices.

Evidence synthesis

Search and extraction. We searched PubMed (267 records; 7 July 2026) and Embase (785 records; 8 October 2026) for studies of host genetic variants and invasive fungal disease. After 221 duplicates were removed, 831 records were screened on title and abstract and 750 were excluded. Eighty-one reports were sought for retrieval. Eighteen could not be retrieved (twelve had no full text available, and for six the link pointed to the wrong document), and 63 were assessed in full. Three were excluded: a functional genomics study with no clinical outcome, a paediatric acute myeloid leukaemia study that did not analyse genotype against the fungal endpoint, and a study that compared patients with leukaemia against healthy controls without analysing infection by genotype. Sixty studies were included (Figure 1).

The extraction yielded 2,008 variant rows. Of these, 970 were usable: 823 effect estimates with both confidence limits and an orientable effect allele, 115 complete 2×3 genotype tables and 32 complete 2×2 tables. [Describe the extraction process and the round-2 human verification.]

Every row whose comparison text had no reference marker was checked against the source tables. That covered seven studies. The two reversals found (12,13) and one duplicated row read in the opposite direction (4) were corrected and logged with their evidence (43 corrected rows in all).

Panel. Candidate variants needed at least three meta-analysable studies. Within each gene, pairwise linkage was measured in phased 1000 Genomes phase 3 European haplotypes (503 samples) (14). Haplotype frequencies were rescaled to gnomAD v4 non-Finnish European allele frequencies (15) by iterative proportional fitting. Linked SNPs were collapsed into one haplotype term per gene. Each term is an additive per-copy effect of a risk haplotype class:

  • PTX3: h2, the class carrying A at rs2305619 (tagged equally by rs1840680), frequency 48.2%.
  • CLEC7A: the Y238X haplotype, the C allele of rs16910526, which lies only on one rs7309123 background, frequency 7.7%.
  • TLR4: the G-T haplotype of rs4986790 and rs4986791, frequency 6.1%. It was pooled under a carrier model with a continuity correction, because no cohort had minor homozygotes.
  • CD209: chromosomes carrying G at rs4804800, frequency 12.5%.

TLR9 rs352140 was dropped because only one study was usable. IL10 rs1800896 and IFNG rs2069705 were dropped because they had one and two studies respectively, and the two IFNG studies disagreed in direction. One study (12) was excluded from the primary analysis because its main endpoint included colonisation.

Mapping and pooling. Each study’s contrast (allelic, dominant, recessive or genotype counts) was converted to a log odds ratio per copy of the risk class. The conversion solves a haplotype model: additive on the log-odds scale, haplotype pairs in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, case–control sampling. Standard errors come from the delta method. One effect was taken per study. The primary analysis used recipient genotype, the odds-ratio scale and whole-cohort analyses. Hazard-ratio studies were added only as a sensitivity analysis, or as a labelled fallback where fewer than three odds-ratio studies existed. Effects were pooled with a Bayesian random-effects model, μ ~ N(0, 1) and τ ~ half-normal(0.5), evaluated on a grid.

Second CLEC7A parameter. The three CLEC7A haplotypes carry a second effect: the A-G haplotype (G at rs7309123 on the non-Y238X background) against A-C. rs7309123 is a C/G palindrome. Its strand was therefore resolved study by study, from control allele frequencies, primer sequences and full texts. The primary estimate uses the three strand-resolved studies of European or Turkish cohorts (4,7,8). Two East Asian studies (16,17) were mapped through 1000 Genomes East Asian haplotype frequencies and entered a sensitivity analysis. This parameter was not part of the pre-specified four-term panel. The four-term analysis is therefore primary, and the five-term analysis is secondary.

The genetic layer

Each patient receives one genotype, which is shared by all of their admissions. The dosage of each risk class (0, 1 or 2) follows Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium at the gnomAD frequencies. Genotype is independent across terms and of the clinical profile. The true risk of an admission is

\[\operatorname{logit} p = \eta_X + \sum_j \beta_j g_j + a_X,\]

where \[\eta_X\] is the baseline linear predictor of clinical profile \[X\] (one of 128) and \[g_j\] the dosage of term \[j\]. The intercept \[a_X\] is chosen separately for each profile so that the risk averaged over genotypes equals the clinical score’s risk,

\[\sum_g \pi_g \operatorname{expit}\!\left(\eta_X + \beta^\top g + a_X\right) = \operatorname{expit}(\eta_X),\]

where \[\pi_g\] is the Hardy–Weinberg probability of genotype \[g\]. The conditional effect of genotype is the same in every profile; what is held fixed is that the clinical score is exactly calibrated within every clinical profile. A change in net benefit therefore cannot come from correcting miscalibration of the clinical score, and reflects the information in genotype. We call this the profile-preserving truth. Each genetic score is built in the same way, with an intercept per profile that holds its mean risk at \[\operatorname{expit}(\eta_X)\] (for a score with estimated weights simulated on a sample of genotypes, the mean over that sample).

The first version of the layer (D5) used one intercept \[a\] for all profiles, which holds the overall prevalence at 3.26% but not the risk within each profile. Because \[\operatorname{E}_g\{\operatorname{expit}(\eta_X + \beta^\top g + a)\} \neq \operatorname{expit}(\eta_X)\] (Jensen’s inequality), the clinical score is then miscalibrated within profiles under the simulated truth, and some of a genetic score’s gain could be the correction of that miscalibration. We keep this global-intercept truth as a sensitivity analysis and compare three things (addendum 24):

  • current: the global-intercept truth, with the clinical score \[\operatorname{expit}(\eta_X)\] as the comparator (the construction of the first version);
  • marginal: the same truth, with the best clinical-only rule under it as the comparator, the marginal risk \[m(X) = \sum_g \pi_g \operatorname{expit}(\eta_X + \beta^\top g + a)\];
  • profile: the profile-preserving truth, with \[\operatorname{expit}(\eta_X)\] as the comparator, which is then exactly calibrated.

Which comparison answers the clinical question. The question is whether adding genotype to a clinical score that is already calibrated would change decisions for patients stratified by that score. The profile comparison estimates this. The current comparison handicaps the clinical score by construction. The marginal comparison removes that handicap while keeping the truth, so the difference between current and marginal measures the part of the gain that is a correction of induced miscalibration, and the difference between marginal and profile measures the effect of the truth itself being a different simulated population.

True effects \[\beta_j\] were drawn 2,000 times from each term’s pooled posterior under two readings:

  • mean: the pooled mean μ;
  • predictive: a new-study effect drawn from N(μ, τ), which carries between-study heterogeneity.

Three scores were compared under the same truth:

  • the clinical score alone;
  • the ceiling score, which uses the true effects (the best any genetic score could do);
  • the realised score, which uses the pooled posterior medians, recentred.

Net benefit at threshold \[p_t\] is

\[\text{NB}(p_t) = \frac{\text{TP}}{n} - \frac{\text{FP}}{n}\cdot\frac{p_t}{1-p_t}.\]

The gain is the net benefit of a genetic score minus that of the clinical score. It was computed exactly over the 128 clinical profiles and all genotype combinations (81 for four terms), with no patient-level sampling. Under the global-intercept truth, a patient-level simulation on a 100-fold replicated cohort reproduced the exact gain (0.00041 against 0.00040 at 5%).

MCID. Before any genetic result, the minimum clinically important difference was defined as 20% more true cases at no increase in false positives. That is \[0.2 \times \text{TP}_{\text{clin}}/n\]. Under the profile-preserving truth the clinical score’s true positives are exactly those of the refit baseline, so the MCID is unchanged (+0.00458 at 5%, +0.00160 at 20%). The 5% threshold, the high-risk cut-off of the original score, is primary. The 20% threshold is secondary and does not enter the headline conclusion. We report three quantities:

  • the median gain over draws, with its 90% range;
  • the extra true cases per 1,000 admissions;
  • the share of draws in which the gain reaches the MCID.

Secondary and sensitivity analyses

All of these were run under the profile-preserving truth. The global-intercept versions are kept as sensitivity analyses (addendum 25).

  • Sampling variability. We simulated 2,000 studies of the cohort’s size per scenario. Each study resampled patients with their admissions, assigned genotypes, drew outcomes from the true risk, and recorded the observed gain of fixed scores.

  • Robustness. The layer was repeated in four further settings, each with its own MCID:

    • the 150 correlation-ensemble members;
    • the published equation on the 4,078 admissions;
    • the published equation on the 4,127 rows;
    • a refit on the 4,127 rows.

  • Attribution. The gain was divided among the terms by Shapley decomposition. The value of a subset is the gain of a score carrying only those terms, under the full truth. Shapley values sum to the total gain, but net benefit is not additive in the terms, so the split is a fair division of the gain and not a set of separable effects.

  • Effect modification by neutropenia. This tests the hypothesis that per-copy effects are larger in neutropenic admissions (H_F, from the inflammatory-levelling argument of Feys and colleagues (18)). The direction we expected was recorded before the run.

    • Strata: admissions were split by the prolonged-neutropenia predictor.
    • Truth: stratum-specific effects were constrained to average, weighted by events, to the pooled effect. The ratio of the neutropenic to the non-neutropenic effect was set to ∞, 4, 1, 1/4 and 0. A further scenario applied the stratum effects of the only stratified study (5) directly.
    • Scores: an oracle score, a common score with the pooled estimates, and two scores that assume a direction.
    • Interaction test: in 1,000 simulated studies per scenario, a logistic model with the baseline logit as an offset estimated the interaction between the genetic score and neutropenia.

  • Donor genotype. In the allogeneic transplant stratum (272 admissions, 43 events, from an admission-level flag), donor effects from the donor-genotype studies were tested against the stratum’s own MCID. The baseline score has no allogeneic item and under-predicts in the stratum, so the truth holds, within each profile, the risk that includes the stratum’s recalibration offset, and the scores hold the baseline’s risk.

  • Architecture sweep. The sweep covered k independent loci with the same risk-allele frequency and the same per-allele odds ratio:

    • k from 1 to 20;
    • odds ratio from 1.1 to 3.0 by 0.1;
    • frequency from 0.05 to 0.50 by 0.05.

    That gives 4,000 architectures, computed exactly by convolution with the ceiling score. In a realised version, each locus weight carried independent error with standard deviation σ from 0.05 to 0.40, with 12 replicate discovery studies per architecture.

  • Pathogen specificity. The sweep treats every case as genotype-sensitive. In a further scenario only a fraction f of cases (the target pathogen, for example Aspergillus among all invasive mould disease) arises through the pathway the loci act on and the rest are independent of genotype. The true risk is (1 − f) times the clinical risk plus f times the risk with the genetic effect, with an intercept per clinical profile on the genetic part, so that the clinical score stays calibrated in every profile. We assumed f = 1, 0.8, 0.6 and 0.4, because the cohort has no pathogen-level outcome. Two ceiling scores were compared: weights equal to the target-pathogen effect (what a pathogen-specific discovery study would estimate) and weights fitted to the all-mould outcome (what a study of any mould disease would estimate). For f = 0.8 and 0.6 the weights also carried independent error with σ = 0.10, 0.20 and 0.40, with 12 replicate discovery studies per architecture. The grid had odds ratios from 1.2 to 3.0 (10 values), frequencies of 0.1, 0.3 and 0.5, and 1 to 20 loci.

  • Stress tests of the realised score. At f = 1 and 0.8, with σ = 0.10 and 0.20 and odds ratios of 2.0, 2.5 and 3.0, we added three departures from the idealised score, each alone and all together: loci with different frequencies and so different precision; the winner’s curse, with the k loci picked by their z-scores from five times as many candidates, the rest null; and incomplete tagging, with the scored variant correlated 0.7 with the causal one (a separate sweep used 0.5, 0.7, 0.9 and 1.0). The causal loci themselves were also linked in a further sweep: blocks of four adjacent loci with allele correlations of 0.3 and 0.6, with the weights estimated for each locus on its own, as in a single-SNP discovery study, or jointly for the block. All these departures were then applied to the main grid at f = 1 (600 architectures, σ = 0.10 and 0.40).

  • Net reclassification improvement (tertiary). This analysis places the earlier 22.5% figure in context. NRI was computed exactly in three forms:

    • categorical at 5%;
    • three categories, with cuts at 5% and 20%;
    • continuous, in which any change in predicted risk counts as a reclassification.

    Five scores were compared with the clinical score:

    • the four-term panel, with true effects or pooled estimates;
    • the PTX3 h2 term alone;
    • a score that adds the PTX3 effect once for each of the three SNPs, applied to real, linked chromosomes;
    • the earlier analysis’s structure, in which the three SNPs are simulated independently and the effect enters both the truth and the score.

    The earlier per-SNP effects are not on record, so every PTX3 structure used the pooled per-copy effect, over the same 2,000 posterior draws as the main analysis. We also traced NRI against the per-copy effect from a log odds ratio of 0 to 1.6.

Results

Cohort and clinical baseline

The base cohort has 4,078 admissions in 1,945 patients (2.10 admissions per patient) and 133 events (3.26%). The refit baseline’s odds ratios ranged from 2.15 for uncontrolled malignancy to 7.28 for prior mould disease. Every published coefficient lies inside the refit’s 90% bootstrap interval. The aROC was 0.858 apparent and 0.853 after optimism correction, and the calibration slope was 0.97. On the same admissions, the published equation under-predicted slightly (3.06% against 3.26%).

At the 5% threshold the refit baseline flags 15.7% of admissions, with 22.9 true and 133.8 false positives per 1,000. Its net benefit is 0.0158. The MCID is therefore +0.00458 (patient bootstrap 90% interval 0.00387 to 0.00566), or 4.6 extra true cases per 1,000 admissions at no extra false positives. At 20% the MCID is +0.00160. Under the published equation the MCIDs would be +0.00404 and +0.00113.

Evidence base

Table 1 shows the pooled effects, and Figure 2 the study effects behind them. Each term rests on three to five studies. CD209 is the only term in the four-term panel whose interval excludes an odds ratio of 1. PTX3 is borderline, with a 5.5% posterior probability that its effect is below zero. CLEC7A Y238X is centred below 1. The second CLEC7A parameter, A-G against A-C, is estimated at an odds ratio of 1.86 per copy. Its interval excludes 1 under every strand assumption (1.5 to 1.9).

The two East Asian studies point the other way (difference from the European estimate −1.19 on the log scale, SE 0.31, P = 0.0001). Adding them gives 1.28 (0.79 to 1.95). The linkage of rs7309123 with rs3901533 in phased 1000 Genomes data explains this pattern: it is the reversal expected when a tag SNP sits on different haplotype backgrounds in the two ancestries.

Does the construction of the truth favour the genetic score?

The clinical score is exactly calibrated within every profile under the profile-preserving truth, by construction. Under the global-intercept truth it was not: for the four-term panel (pooled-mean reading) the median calibration slope of the clinical score was 0.98, and its largest error within a profile was 0.023 in risk (0.029 under the predictive reading). The best clinical-only rule under that truth, the marginal risk, did better than the clinical score in 0.05% to 34% of draws, depending on panel, reading and threshold. The margin was at most +0.00027 in net benefit in any draw, and the 95th percentile of its share of the ceiling gain was at most 0.1%. The median gain was therefore the same with the marginal risk as the comparator as with the clinical score (Table 3). Correcting induced miscalibration did not produce the reported gains.

Moving from the global-intercept to the profile-preserving truth did change the results, because it is a different simulated population. The next four sections (sampling and robustness, attribution, effect modification, donor genotype) use the profile-preserving truth. Rerunning them under the global-intercept truth gave the same conclusions. Most gains and shares differed by a few per cent. Which terms carry the gain

In the four-term panel, CD209 supplied 51.4% of the Shapley gain at 5% (ceiling, pooled mean). PTX3 supplied 22.6%, TLR4 19.0% and CLEC7A 7.0% (Figure 5). CD209 has the largest pooled effect but rests on three studies. With the second CLEC7A parameter the ranking changed. CLEC7A supplied 50.9%, almost all of it through the common A-G haplotype (47.0%), and CD209 fell to 26.9%, PTX3 to 12.4% and TLR4 to 9.8%. CLEC7A’s share stayed between 44.8% and 53.3% in every combination of cohort, correlation structure, reading, score and threshold. It was the largest single contributor in 54% to 70% of draws.

Effect modification by neutropenia

Neutropenic admissions were 28% of the cohort (1,157), with an event rate of 9.2% and 106 of the 133 events (80%). With homogeneous effects, the gain per admission was 11 times larger in neutropenic admissions (+0.00160 against +0.00015 at 5%). That stratum supplied 80% of the whole-cohort gain at 5% and 95% at 20%. With five terms it supplied 82% and 95%, and 77% to 83% at 5% across every sensitivity setting.

The only stratified evidence (5) points against H_F. Mapped to the per-copy PTX3 h2 scale, its effect was +1.17 in non-neutropenic and −0.13 in neutropenic courses for rs2305619 (difference −1.30, SE 0.72, P = 0.07). For rs3816527 the difference was −1.99 (SE 1.01, P = 0.049). These estimates come from 212 courses and 26 events, with neutropenia defined at presentation.

Genetic architectures that would reach the MCID

Under the profile-preserving truth, of the 4,000 architectures 40% reached the 5% MCID and 77% reached the 20% MCID (Figure 7). The pattern was:

  • One locus: never enough. At an odds ratio of 3.0 and frequency 0.5 it reached 37% of the MCID.
  • Odds ratio 1.5 or less: no number of independent loci up to 20 reached it at 5% (linked causal loci can; see below).
  • Odds ratio 2.0: 9 to 12 loci were needed at frequencies of 0.2 to 0.5, and 19 at 0.1.
  • Odds ratio 3.0: 4 to 5 loci were enough at frequencies of 0.15 to 0.5.

The gain peaks at a frequency of about 0.45 and is slightly lower at 0.5, so the frontier is not strictly monotone in frequency near 0.5 (under the global-intercept truth, for example, an odds ratio of 2.9 needs 3 loci at frequencies of 0.4 and 0.45 and 4 at 0.5).

The global-intercept truth needed 7 to 9 loci at odds ratio 2.0 and 3 to 4 at odds ratio 3.0, so the 5% frontier moved out by two to three loci at an odds ratio of 2.0, and by two at 1.8 (frequency 0.3; Table 3). Under that truth one architecture with an odds ratio of 1.5 (20 loci at frequency 0.45) reached the 5% MCID, by +0.000007. The profile-preserving truth removes it.

The four-term panel corresponds to about 7 loci of odds ratio 1.3 at frequency 0.3, and would need to be roughly eight times stronger to reach the 5% MCID. Architectures near the frontier depended on the unpublished correlation structure (gain over MCID varied by about 15% across ensemble members, computed under the global-intercept truth). Those well inside or outside it did not.

With estimated weights, a score kept a median of 96% of the ceiling gain at a per-locus precision of σ = 0.10. It kept 89% at σ = 0.20 and 58% at σ = 0.40, which is the range of precision of the present pooled estimates (0.18 to 0.38). At those precisions, between 12% and 36% of the architectures that reached the MCID with exact weights no longer did. The loci needed for a given odds ratio rose by one to several. At σ = 0.40 and an odds ratio of 1.8 (frequency 0.3), no number of loci up to 20 reached the 5% MCID under the profile-preserving truth, against 20 under the global-intercept truth.

Discussion

Principal findings

On the published evidence, adding the four best-evidenced host variants to a validated clinical score for invasive mould disease would change few decisions at the 5% threshold. With the true effects in the score, the panel found about one extra case per 1,000 admissions, against a pre-specified bar of 4.6. Every combination of evidence reading, cohort, baseline equation and correlation structure we examined gave the same conclusion. The most favourable addition the evidence allows, a second CLEC7A parameter, still left the gain at about a quarter of the MCID.

The conclusion does not depend on how the genetic truth was added to the clinical model. The first version of the simulation held only the overall prevalence, which left the clinical score slightly miscalibrated within profiles. That raised the gains at 5% by 5% to 6% and made the architecture frontier two to three loci easier to reach, but none of the gain was a correction of the miscalibration: the best clinical-only rule under that truth did as well as the clinical score to within 0.00027 in net benefit in every draw. We therefore report the profile-preserving truth, under which the clinical score is calibrated by construction and a gain can only come from the information in genotype. That is the comparison that corresponds to the clinical question, which is whether genotype adds to a score that is already well calibrated.

A real gain of this size would also be hard to observe: it would show as no gain, or a loss, in a fifth to a third of cohorts the size of the derivation cohort. Taken together, the panel behaves like about seven loci of odds ratio 1.3, far from the architectures the sweep shows would be needed.

Comparison with the earlier reclassification result

An earlier, unpublished analysis of the same question reported a net reclassification improvement (NRI) of 22.5% from adding PTX3 variants to the same clinical score. Our results differ from it for two reasons.

Linkage. The earlier analysis simulated rs2305619, rs3816527 and rs1840680 as independent multiplicative risks, although in European haplotypes they tag one haplotype class (r² 0.79 to 0.996, D′ = 1). Its homozygote odds ratio of 15.2 for rs1840680 came from that triple counting. It also used the global dbSNP frequency of rs2305619 (0.36) rather than the non-Finnish European one (0.48), which shrinks the homozygote class from 21% to 13% of the cohort. At the same per-copy effect, the independent structure quadrupled the net-benefit gain (+0.00033 against +0.00008; Figure 9). Treated correctly as one haplotype, PTX3 adds +0.00008 in net benefit at 5%, about 2% of the MCID. It does not reach the MCID at any per-copy odds ratio up to 5.

The metric. NRI counts movements across risk categories without weighing the consequences of the decisions they imply. It can be positive for uninformative markers, and it can reward miscalibration (19–21). Our own four-term haplotype panel had a continuous NRI of 34%, above the earlier 22.5%, with a net-benefit gain an eighth of the MCID. An NRI of this size is therefore no evidence that decisions would change.

Genotype does improve the ranking (aROC +0.012), but few admissions cross the threshold. The extra admissions flagged at 5% included about nine false positives for every extra true case (8.9 under the profile-preserving truth). At this threshold a true case is worth up to 19 false positives, so these moves are beneficial on balance, but there are too few of them to matter.

Continuous NRI also failed to register harm. The three-SNP score applied to real, linked chromosomes lowered net benefit yet had a positive continuous NRI (29%), and the measure’s value and components swung with tiny shifts in heterozygotes’ predicted risk: for the correct h2 term it was 13% under one construction of the truth and 34% under the other, with the same net-benefit gain. Net benefit at a clinically chosen threshold, judged against a difference fixed in advance, asks the question a clinician would ask: does the score find more patients who will develop disease without treating more who will not (9)?

Where genetic information might matter

The analyses point to four settings where genotype could carry more value. Each comes with qualifications.

At a higher threshold. At the secondary 20% threshold, fewer admissions are flagged and each extra true case is worth fewer false positives. There the five-term ceiling score reached the MCID in 64% of draws. This result is secondary, rests on a parameter estimated from three studies, and should be read as a hypothesis.

In neutropenic admissions. Neutropenic admissions hold 80% of the events. They supplied about four-fifths of the gain whichever way the effect modification ran, unless the effect was placed entirely in non-neutropenic admissions. The only stratified data (5) point the opposite way to the inflammatory-levelling hypothesis. They come from one small study with a different definition of neutropenia, so they do not settle the question.

The design analysis shows what a genotyped cohort of this size could do, provided genotype is measured and the weights are right. With the five-term score, it would detect a modification of the size the inflammatory-levelling argument implies in 60% to 89% of studies, and a modification of Brunel’s size almost always. Of the follow-up studies these results suggest, this one would be the most informative. It would test the biology directly. It would also show whether a single pooled score is safe to use: under Brunel’s pattern, such a score would do harm.

With larger effects or more loci. Reaching the frontier needs loci with odds ratios of 2 or more, or many loci with precise weights. A per-locus precision of 0.1 on the log scale needs a discovery sample of about 1,000 balanced subjects per locus at a common frequency. A cohort the size of the Bologna one gives 0.14 to 0.22. To our knowledge no genome-wide association study of invasive aspergillosis has been published. The candidate-gene literature cannot supply the number of precisely estimated loci the sweep shows would be needed. These requirements also assume that every case responds to the loci. If only some of the moulds do, they rise sharply: at 80% the loci needed roughly doubled, and at 60% or less no architecture in the grid reached the 5% MCID. Variants specific to one pathogen are therefore a weak basis for a score that decides for all moulds, and better suited to a pathogen-specific decision. The requirements also assume that the scored variants are the causal ones. A tag correlated 0.7 with the causal variant kept under half of the gain, and then no number of loci up to 20 reached the 5% MCID at an odds ratio of 2.0; a tag correlated 0.9 cost a fifth of the gain. The frontier is a statement about independent, causal, well-measured loci.

In allogeneic transplant recipients. Here the biological case for donor genotype is strongest (3). But the baseline already flags 77% of these admissions at 5%, which leaves little room for genotype to add. At 20% donor genotype could plausibly reach the stratum’s MCID. The evidence is one or two studies per term, however, and none for CD209.

Strengths and limitations

The study has six main strengths:

  • The MCID, the primary threshold and the panel were fixed before any genetic result existed and locked in version control. Every later change is a dated addendum that discloses what had been seen.
  • Gains were computed exactly rather than by Monte Carlo sampling.
  • Linked SNPs were modelled as haplotypes.
  • Every primary-analysis contrast with an ambiguous reference group was checked against its source, and errors were corrected and logged. The predictions for the neutropenia analysis were committed before it was run.
  • The clinical baseline comes from individual admission-level data rather than published margins.
  • The code, tests and summary outputs are public.

The limitations fall into seven groups.

The evidence is thin. Each term rests on three to five studies. Some cohorts overlap, notably the Spanish registry series and the Jena AML cohorts, and that overlap has only partly been adjudicated. The record of the human round-2 verification is not yet in the repository. The direction of one donor study’s recessive PTX3 rows (Fisher 2017) is inferred from an allele-order footnote (addenda 39 and 40). Small candidate-gene studies are prone to publication bias and inflated effects. If so, our pooled effects are optimistic, which would strengthen the negative conclusion at 5% rather than weaken it.

The mapping rests on assumptions. Converting heterogeneous contrasts to one per-copy scale assumes additive effects and Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. It also uses European haplotype frequencies for every cohort except the two East Asian studies in the CLEC7A sensitivity analysis. The priors on μ and τ, and the continuity correction for TLR4, are analytic choices with little data to check them against.

Several simulation assumptions favour the genetic score. The realised score uses posterior medians, which are already shrunk, and it ignores the winner’s curse and the incomplete tagging of causal variants. In stress tests on odds ratios of 2 or more, the winner’s curse and unequal precision changed the loci needed little, but a tag correlated 0.7 with the causal variant kept under half of the gain (addendum 42). Real-world realised gains would therefore be lower.

Other simulation assumptions could cut either way. Genotype is assumed independent across genes and independent of the clinical profile. The analysis considers one threshold decision per admission. Only candidate genes are modelled.

The construction of the genetic truth. The profile-preserving truth adds the same logit-scale effect in every clinical profile and compensates with an intercept per profile. Other forms of interaction between genotype and clinical risk were not examined. The primary truth was adopted after the global-intercept results were known (addendum 24), though it was not tuned to them. Every analysis in this paper, including the sampling-variability, correlation-ensemble, attribution, effect-modification, donor-stratum and robustness analyses, was run under it, and the first version’s global intercept was kept as a sensitivity analysis (addenda 24 to 34). Compared with that sensitivity analysis, the global intercept overstated gains at 5% by 5% to 6% in the panels, let 241 of 4,000 architectures reach the MCID that did not under the profile-preserving truth, and made the scenario with all of the effect in non-neutropenic admissions look more favourable (the oracle score reached the 5% MCID in 18% of draws against 2%). It changed little at 20% and did not change the neutropenia pattern, the attribution or the donor result. The non-neutropenic scenario is the one most sensitive to the construction because it puts a per-copy effect about five times the pooled one into a low-risk stratum. There the single intercept flattens the clinical gradient: the lowest-risk profiles, which hold most of the stratum, get a true risk 14% to 29% above the clinical score’s, and the highest-risk ones a much lower one. This is a different simulated population, not a correction of the comparator: against the best clinical-only rule the gain under the global intercept was almost the same (3.01 against 3.05, per 1,000 admissions at 5% and the pooled estimates). The gain was 11% lower under the profile-preserving truth, which is enough to move the MCID from the 82nd to above the 95th percentile of the draws (addendum 26). The other scenarios would be distorted too at effects that large, by 11% to 28% in the median gain and more where the large effect is in the neutropenic stratum, but none of them has one: their stratum effects are at most 2.5 times the pooled one, and they differ by 3% to 8% (addendum 27). The five-term panel and the realised score behave in the same way, apart from the realised score in the scenario with all of the effect in non-neutropenic admissions, where its gain is negligible under both constructions (addendum 28). The reason is that the global intercept raises the true risk of the lowest-risk profiles and lowers it in the higher-risk ones: that helps a score strong enough to flag the former, as the ceiling score is, and hurts one that reaches only the latter, as the pooled weights of the realised score do (addenda 30 to 32, which test it by changing the truth band by band, for four and five terms). The distortion exceeds 10% only when the panel’s effects are 2.4 to 2.7 times the pooled ones (1.6 to 1.8 times for five terms), or when a single dominant term has a per-copy odds ratio of 3 to 5.5; with every term at the 95th percentile of its posterior it is 9% (four terms) and 12% (five terms) at 5% and 0.5% to 2% at 20% (addendum 29).

The second CLEC7A parameter is the least mature part of the evidence. It comes from three studies. The opposite direction in East Asian studies fits a tag SNP whose linkage to a causal variant differs between ancestries, but the causal variant has not been identified.

The cohort is a single pre-pandemic centre. Admissions run from 2007 to 2016, and the outcome is all invasive moulds, not aspergillosis alone. The baseline is evaluated on the data it was fitted to, although the optimism was small (0.005 in aROC). Prophylaxis practice has changed since 2016, and the score’s calibration today is unknown.

The decision framework is narrow. Net benefit at a fixed threshold captures one use of a risk score. The MCID of 20% more true cases is a judgement, though the strictest of the three readings we considered. Other uses of genotype, such as choosing an antifungal agent or enriching a trial, were not evaluated.

The stress tests of locus-specific precision, the winner’s curse, incomplete tagging and linkage among the causal loci were run within the pathogen-specificity grid and on the main grid at f = 1, one setting each (blocks of four loci for linkage). The pathogen-specificity scenario rests on an assumed share f of cases due to the target pathogen, because the cohort has no pathogen-level outcome.

The NRI comparison rests on the pooled PTX3 effect, because the earlier analysis’s per-SNP inputs are not on record, and we do not know which form of NRI it reported.

Conclusions

On the published evidence, host genotype at PTX3, CLEC7A, TLR4 and CD209 is unlikely to change decisions at the 5% threshold for invasive mould disease in haematological inpatients. A study the size of the derivation cohort could not reliably detect the gain it would give. Genotyping would become worth doing only with variants of larger effect, or many more of them, than candidate-gene studies have found. The architecture frontier behind that statement assumes independent, causal, well-measured loci that act on all the moulds in the outcome. It moves out when only some of the moulds respond or when the scored variant only tags the causal one (a correlation of 0.7 kept under half of the gain), and it moves in only if distinct causal loci are linked, so its numbers are conditional on those assumptions. A more immediate use for a genotyped cohort of this size would be to test whether genetic effects depend on neutropenia, which would also show whether a single pooled score is safe.

## Abstract

**Background.** Variants in *PTX3*, *CLEC7A*, *TLR4* and *CD209* are often reported to be associated with invasive aspergillosis, and adding genotype to clinical risk scores has been proposed clinical management. Whether incorporation of genetic risk factors would change clinical decisions has not been tested.

**Methods.** In a pre-specified simulation study, we added host genotype to an established 60-day clinical risk score for proven or probable invasive mould disease (IMD) in patients with hematological malignancies. The original clinical risk score was based on 4,078 haematology admissions in 1,945 patients (Bologna, 2007–2016; 133 events). Effects for four haplotype terms were deverived from a systematic review (60 studies included) and pooled with Bayesian random-effects models. The effects of genetic mutations were added to the clinical logit with an intercept for each clinical profile, so that the clinical score stayed calibrated and any gain reflected the genetic information. Net-benefit gains were computed exactly for 2,000 posterior draws. The primary endpoint was the gain in identifying patients at a 5% risk threshold beyong the clinical risk score alone. It was judged against a pre-specified minimum clinically important difference (MCID) of 20% more true cases at no extra false positives.

**Results.** Pooled odds ratios per copy of the risk haplotype ranged from 0.84 (CLEC7A Y238X) to 1.80 (CD209). With the true effects in the score, the four-term panel raised net benefit at 5% by a median of +0.00056 (90% range 0.00015 to 0.00127). That is 1.1 extra true cases per 1,000 admissions, against 4.6 required. At most 0.6% of draws reached the MCID, although the area under the ROC curve rose from 0.861 to 0.873 and the continuous net reclassification improvement was 34%. A second CLEC7A haplotype doubled the gain to +0.00115, about a quarter of the MCID. Neutropenic admissions (80% of events) supplied about four-fifths of the gain. No panel of up to 20 independent loci with per-allele odds ratios of 1.5 or less reached the MCID, and a scored variant correlated 0.7 with the causal one kept under half of the gain. If only 80% of cases were genotype-sensitive, reaching the MCID took about twice as many loci (for example, 19 to 20 rather than 10 at an odds ratio of 2.0), and at 60% or less no architecture in the grid reached it. A single global intercept, used in an earlier version of the simulation, gave gains 5% to 6% larger at 5%, and comparison with the correctly marginalised clinical risk showed that this was not a correction of miscalibration.

**Conclusions.** On the published evidence, genotype at these genes is unlikely to change decisions at the 5% threshold in haematological inpatients. Doing so would need much larger effects, or many more loci, than candidate-gene studies report. A genotyped, neutropenia-stratified cohort of this size could test whether genetic effects depend on the inflammatory state of the host.

## Introduction

Invasive mould disease, mostly invasive aspergillosis, remains a major cause of morbidity and death in patients with haematological malignancies [@pagano2017]. Decisions about antifungal prophylaxis, diagnostic work-up and pre-emptive treatment depends on the estiamted risk of an indiviudal patient for developing invasive mold disease (IMD). Clinical risk scores have been proposed to facilitate individualised risk estaimtes for patients. Stanzani and colleagues derived a score from seven routinely recorded host factors in 4,127 admissions to the haematology unit in Bologna [@stanzani2019]. It reached an aROC of 0.85 for proven or probable IMD within 60 days and differentiated high-risk populations at a predicted probability of 5%.

Host genetics might add information that clinical factors do not capture. Candidate-gene studies have reported associations between invasive aspergillosis and variants in genes of innate antifungal immunity: the long pentraxin PTX3, the β-glucan receptor dectin-1 (*CLEC7A*), Toll-like receptor 4 and DC-SIGN (*CD209*) [@cunha2014; @sainz2012; @brunel2018; @fisher2017; @kalkanci2020; @schnetzke2024]. After allogeneic transplantation, the reconstituted innate immune system is donor-derived, which has led to proposals to use donor genotype to stratify transplant recipients [@cunha2014]. These findings have prompted proposals for genetically augmented risk scores.

Two problems make such proposals difficult to clinically implement. First, the evidence on genetic risk factors for IMD is still relatively small and heterogeneous. Linked variants in the same gene are often reported as separate findings, and adding them to a model as independent terms counts the same signal more than once. Second, gains in discrimination or reclassification do not show whether anyone would be managed differently. Decision-curve analysis is one approach that can be used to address this issue, as it counts true and false positives at a clinically chosen risk threshold, weighted by the harm-to-benefit ratio that the threshold implies [@vickers2006].

We asked how large, how common, and how independent host genetic effects would need to be to change clinical decisions about IMD in patients treated for haematological malignancies, and whether the published evidence supports this thresholds. Our objectives were to (i) estimate the clinical baseline in the derivation cohort and compare it with the published equation; (ii) quantify the decision value of the best-evidenced variants, using effects pooled from the literature; (iii) test whether genetic effects are larger in neutropenic admissions, as the inflammatory-levelling hypothesis predicts if genotype matters most when the host has no strong inflammatory response;and (iv) identify the genetic architectures- i.e. how many risk variants there are, how strong each one is (odds ratio per allele), and how common each one is (risk-allele frequency)- that would produce a important changes in risk assessment beyond clinical factors is the risk score.

## Methods

### Design and pre-specification

This is a simulation study that combines individual admission-level clinical data with a meta-analysis of the published genetic evidence. The design is summarised below in the ADEMP structure for simulation studies [@morris2019]. Design decisions were recorded before any genetic result existed and locked. To add genetic risk to the simulated patients, the original plan adjusted everyone's risk by the same amount, so that the overall rate of IMD stayed at the observed 3.26%. On review, we found that this left the clinical score slightly wrong for some groups of patients with the same clinical risk factors: it under- or over-estimated their risk in the simulated population. Part of any gain from adding genotype could therefore have come from correcting that error, rather than from the genetic information itself. We therefore changed the primary analysis so that risk was adjusted separately within each group of patients with the same clinical risk factors. The clinical score then stays accurate for every group, and any gain can come only from genotype. The original method was kept as a sensitivity analysis. The change lowered the primary gains slightly (by 5% to 6% at the 5% threshold) and did not alter any conclusion, but because it was made after the earlier results had been seen, it was not blind to them. All secondary analyses were rerun under the new method, with the same random seeds and the same draws of the genetic effects.

### Clinical cohort and baseline model

The clinical data are the admission-level derivation data of the Stanzani score, from the BOSCORE registry of the haematology unit in Bologna (admissions 2007–2016). The outcome is proven or probable IMD by EORTC criteria within 60 days of admission. The seven predictors are:

- prior mould disease;
- high-dose corticosteroids;
- uncontrolled malignancy;
- high-risk chemotherapy;
- prolonged neutropenia;
- severe lymphopenia;
- cytomegalovirus infection.

The published analysis file has 4,127 rows. These are exactly the rows of the parent registry extract that are complete on the seven items. Forty-nine admission numbers appeared twice, each time as an event row plus a non-event row with the same patient and predictor profile. We confirmed these pairs as a recording artefact and removed the duplicate row. The base cohort is therefore 4,078 unique admissions in 1,945 patients, with 133 events (3.26%). Only counts and pseudonymised fields were used, and no patient-level data are in the repository (see Ethics and Data availability).

The baseline is a maximum-likelihood logistic regression of the outcome on the seven predictors in the base cohort. Its uncertainty was handled as follows:

- Standard errors are clustered by patient.
- The bootstrap resamples patients and repeats the refit in each of 2,000 resamples.
- Optimism was estimated by Harrell's bootstrap method [@harrell1996]. It is reported, not subtracted.

The baseline is held fixed from here on. The joint distribution of clinical risk is the observed distribution of the 128 possible predictor profiles, 75 of which occur. Three alternatives were kept as sensitivity analyses:

- the published equation;
- the 4,127-row cohort;
- a Gaussian-copula reconstruction of the predictor correlation, fitted to the published marginal counts. It was carried as an ensemble of 150 near-optimal correlation matrices.

### Evidence synthesis

**Search and extraction.** We searched PubMed (267 records; 7 July 2026) and Embase (785 records; 8 October 2026) for studies of host genetic variants and invasive fungal disease. After 221 duplicates were removed, 831 records were screened on title and abstract and 750 were excluded. Eighty-one reports were sought for retrieval. Eighteen could not be retrieved (twelve had no full text available, and for six the link pointed to the wrong document), and 63 were assessed in full. Three were excluded: a functional genomics study with no clinical outcome, a paediatric acute myeloid leukaemia study that did not analyse genotype against the fungal endpoint, and a study that compared patients with leukaemia against healthy controls without analysing infection by genotype. Sixty studies were included (@fig-prisma).

The extraction yielded 2,008 variant rows. Of these, 970 were usable: 823 effect estimates with both confidence limits and an orientable effect allele, 115 complete 2×3 genotype tables and 32 complete 2×2 tables. \[Describe the extraction process and the round-2 human verification.\]

Every row whose comparison text had no reference marker was checked against the source tables. That covered seven studies. The two reversals found [@herrerosanchez2018; @fischer2016] and one duplicated row read in the opposite direction [@sainz2012] were corrected and logged with their evidence (43 corrected rows in all).

**Panel.** Candidate variants needed at least three meta-analysable studies. Within each gene, pairwise linkage was measured in phased 1000 Genomes phase 3 European haplotypes (503 samples) [@genomes2015]. Haplotype frequencies were rescaled to gnomAD v4 non-Finnish European allele frequencies [@gnomad] by iterative proportional fitting. Linked SNPs were collapsed into one haplotype term per gene. Each term is an additive per-copy effect of a risk haplotype class:

- **PTX3:** h2, the class carrying A at rs2305619 (tagged equally by rs1840680), frequency 48.2%.
- **CLEC7A:** the Y238X haplotype, the C allele of rs16910526, which lies only on one rs7309123 background, frequency 7.7%.
- **TLR4:** the G-T haplotype of rs4986790 and rs4986791, frequency 6.1%. It was pooled under a carrier model with a continuity correction, because no cohort had minor homozygotes.
- **CD209:** chromosomes carrying G at rs4804800, frequency 12.5%.

TLR9 rs352140 was dropped because only one study was usable. IL10 rs1800896 and IFNG rs2069705 were dropped because they had one and two studies respectively, and the two IFNG studies disagreed in direction. One study [@herrerosanchez2018] was excluded from the primary analysis because its main endpoint included colonisation.

**Mapping and pooling.** Each study's contrast (allelic, dominant, recessive or genotype counts) was converted to a log odds ratio per copy of the risk class. The conversion solves a haplotype model: additive on the log-odds scale, haplotype pairs in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, case–control sampling. Standard errors come from the delta method. One effect was taken per study. The primary analysis used recipient genotype, the odds-ratio scale and whole-cohort analyses. Hazard-ratio studies were added only as a sensitivity analysis, or as a labelled fallback where fewer than three odds-ratio studies existed. Effects were pooled with a Bayesian random-effects model, μ \~ N(0, 1) and τ \~ half-normal(0.5), evaluated on a grid.

**Second CLEC7A parameter.** The three CLEC7A haplotypes carry a second effect: the A-G haplotype (G at rs7309123 on the non-Y238X background) against A-C. rs7309123 is a C/G palindrome. Its strand was therefore resolved study by study, from control allele frequencies, primer sequences and full texts. The primary estimate uses the three strand-resolved studies of European or Turkish cohorts [@schnetzke2024; @kalkanci2020; @sainz2012]. Two East Asian studies [@chen2019; @tanpaibule2020] were mapped through 1000 Genomes East Asian haplotype frequencies and entered a sensitivity analysis. This parameter was not part of the pre-specified four-term panel. The four-term analysis is therefore primary, and the five-term analysis is secondary.

### The genetic layer

Each patient receives one genotype, which is shared by all of their admissions. The dosage of each risk class (0, 1 or 2) follows Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium at the gnomAD frequencies. Genotype is independent across terms and of the clinical profile. The true risk of an admission is

$$\operatorname{logit} p = \eta_X + \sum_j \beta_j g_j + a_X,$$

where $$\eta_X$$ is the baseline linear predictor of clinical profile $$X$$ (one of 128) and $$g_j$$ the dosage of term $$j$$. The intercept $$a_X$$ is chosen separately for each profile so that the risk averaged over genotypes equals the clinical score's risk,

$$\sum_g \pi_g \operatorname{expit}\!\left(\eta_X + \beta^\top g + a_X\right) = \operatorname{expit}(\eta_X),$$

where $$\pi_g$$ is the Hardy–Weinberg probability of genotype $$g$$. The conditional effect of genotype is the same in every profile; what is held fixed is that the clinical score is exactly calibrated within every clinical profile. A change in net benefit therefore cannot come from correcting miscalibration of the clinical score, and reflects the information in genotype. We call this the **profile-preserving truth**. Each genetic score is built in the same way, with an intercept per profile that holds its mean risk at $$\operatorname{expit}(\eta_X)$$ (for a score with estimated weights simulated on a sample of genotypes, the mean over that sample).

The first version of the layer (D5) used one intercept $$a$$ for all profiles, which holds the overall prevalence at 3.26% but not the risk within each profile. Because $$\operatorname{E}_g\{\operatorname{expit}(\eta_X + \beta^\top g + a)\} \neq
\operatorname{expit}(\eta_X)$$ (Jensen's inequality), the clinical score is then miscalibrated within profiles under the simulated truth, and some of a genetic score's gain could be the correction of that miscalibration. We keep this **global-intercept truth** as a sensitivity analysis and compare three things (addendum 24):

- **current:** the global-intercept truth, with the clinical score $$\operatorname{expit}(\eta_X)$$ as the comparator (the construction of the first version);
- **marginal:** the same truth, with the best clinical-only rule under it as the comparator, the marginal risk $$m(X) = \sum_g \pi_g \operatorname{expit}(\eta_X + \beta^\top g + a)$$;
- **profile:** the profile-preserving truth, with $$\operatorname{expit}(\eta_X)$$ as the comparator, which is then exactly calibrated.

**Which comparison answers the clinical question.** The question is whether adding genotype to a clinical score that is already calibrated would change decisions for patients stratified by that score. The profile comparison estimates this. The current comparison handicaps the clinical score by construction. The marginal comparison removes that handicap while keeping the truth, so the difference between current and marginal measures the part of the gain that is a correction of induced miscalibration, and the difference between marginal and profile measures the effect of the truth itself being a different simulated population.

True effects $$\beta_j$$ were drawn 2,000 times from each term's pooled posterior under two readings:

- **mean:** the pooled mean μ;
- **predictive:** a new-study effect drawn from N(μ, τ), which carries between-study heterogeneity.

Three scores were compared under the same truth:

- the clinical score alone;
- the **ceiling** score, which uses the true effects (the best any genetic score could do);
- the **realised** score, which uses the pooled posterior medians, recentred.

Net benefit at threshold $$p_t$$ is

$$\text{NB}(p_t) = \frac{\text{TP}}{n} - \frac{\text{FP}}{n}\cdot\frac{p_t}{1-p_t}.$$

The gain is the net benefit of a genetic score minus that of the clinical score. It was computed exactly over the 128 clinical profiles and all genotype combinations (81 for four terms), with no patient-level sampling. Under the global-intercept truth, a patient-level simulation on a 100-fold replicated cohort reproduced the exact gain (0.00041 against 0.00040 at 5%).

**MCID.** Before any genetic result, the minimum clinically important difference was defined as 20% more true cases at no increase in false positives. That is $$0.2 \times \text{TP}_{\text{clin}}/n$$. Under the profile-preserving truth the clinical score's true positives are exactly those of the refit baseline, so the MCID is unchanged (+0.00458 at 5%, +0.00160 at 20%). The 5% threshold, the high-risk cut-off of the original score, is primary. The 20% threshold is secondary and does not enter the headline conclusion. We report three quantities:

- the median gain over draws, with its 90% range;
- the extra true cases per 1,000 admissions;
- the share of draws in which the gain reaches the MCID.

### Secondary and sensitivity analyses

All of these were run under the profile-preserving truth. The global-intercept versions are kept as sensitivity analyses (addendum 25).

- **Sampling variability.** We simulated 2,000 studies of the cohort's size per scenario. Each study resampled patients with their admissions, assigned genotypes, drew outcomes from the true risk, and recorded the observed gain of fixed scores.

- **Robustness.** The layer was repeated in four further settings, each with its own MCID:

  - the 150 correlation-ensemble members;
  - the published equation on the 4,078 admissions;
  - the published equation on the 4,127 rows;
  - a refit on the 4,127 rows.

- **Attribution.** The gain was divided among the terms by Shapley decomposition. The value of a subset is the gain of a score carrying only those terms, under the full truth. Shapley values sum to the total gain, but net benefit is not additive in the terms, so the split is a fair division of the gain and not a set of separable effects.

- **Effect modification by neutropenia.** This tests the hypothesis that per-copy effects are larger in neutropenic admissions (H_F, from the inflammatory-levelling argument of Feys and colleagues [@feys2026]). The direction we expected was recorded before the run.

  - **Strata:** admissions were split by the prolonged-neutropenia predictor.
  - **Truth:** stratum-specific effects were constrained to average, weighted by events, to the pooled effect. The ratio of the neutropenic to the non-neutropenic effect was set to ∞, 4, 1, 1/4 and 0. A further scenario applied the stratum effects of the only stratified study [@brunel2018] directly.
  - **Scores:** an oracle score, a common score with the pooled estimates, and two scores that assume a direction.
  - **Interaction test:** in 1,000 simulated studies per scenario, a logistic model with the baseline logit as an offset estimated the interaction between the genetic score and neutropenia.

- **Donor genotype.** In the allogeneic transplant stratum (272 admissions, 43 events, from an admission-level flag), donor effects from the donor-genotype studies were tested against the stratum's own MCID. The baseline score has no allogeneic item and under-predicts in the stratum, so the truth holds, within each profile, the risk that includes the stratum's recalibration offset, and the scores hold the baseline's risk.

- **Architecture sweep.** The sweep covered k independent loci with the same risk-allele frequency and the same per-allele odds ratio:

  - k from 1 to 20;
  - odds ratio from 1.1 to 3.0 by 0.1;
  - frequency from 0.05 to 0.50 by 0.05.

  That gives 4,000 architectures, computed exactly by convolution with the ceiling score. In a realised version, each locus weight carried independent error with standard deviation σ from 0.05 to 0.40, with 12 replicate discovery studies per architecture.

- **Pathogen specificity.** The sweep treats every case as genotype-sensitive. In a further scenario only a fraction f of cases (the target pathogen, for example *Aspergillus* among all invasive mould disease) arises through the pathway the loci act on and the rest are independent of genotype. The true risk is (1 − f) times the clinical risk plus f times the risk with the genetic effect, with an intercept per clinical profile on the genetic part, so that the clinical score stays calibrated in every profile. We assumed f = 1, 0.8, 0.6 and 0.4, because the cohort has no pathogen-level outcome. Two ceiling scores were compared: weights equal to the target-pathogen effect (what a pathogen-specific discovery study would estimate) and weights fitted to the all-mould outcome (what a study of any mould disease would estimate). For f = 0.8 and 0.6 the weights also carried independent error with σ = 0.10, 0.20 and 0.40, with 12 replicate discovery studies per architecture. The grid had odds ratios from 1.2 to 3.0 (10 values), frequencies of 0.1, 0.3 and 0.5, and 1 to 20 loci.

- **Stress tests of the realised score.** At f = 1 and 0.8, with σ = 0.10 and 0.20 and odds ratios of 2.0, 2.5 and 3.0, we added three departures from the idealised score, each alone and all together: loci with different frequencies and so different precision; the winner's curse, with the k loci picked by their z-scores from five times as many candidates, the rest null; and incomplete tagging, with the scored variant correlated 0.7 with the causal one (a separate sweep used 0.5, 0.7, 0.9 and 1.0). The causal loci themselves were also linked in a further sweep: blocks of four adjacent loci with allele correlations of 0.3 and 0.6, with the weights estimated for each locus on its own, as in a single-SNP discovery study, or jointly for the block. All these departures were then applied to the main grid at f = 1 (600 architectures, σ = 0.10 and 0.40).

- **Net reclassification improvement (tertiary).** This analysis places the earlier 22.5% figure in context. NRI was computed exactly in three forms:

  - categorical at 5%;
  - three categories, with cuts at 5% and 20%;
  - continuous, in which any change in predicted risk counts as a reclassification.

  Five scores were compared with the clinical score:

  - the four-term panel, with true effects or pooled estimates;
  - the PTX3 h2 term alone;
  - a score that adds the PTX3 effect once for each of the three SNPs, applied to real, linked chromosomes;
  - the earlier analysis's structure, in which the three SNPs are simulated independently and the effect enters both the truth and the score.

  The earlier per-SNP effects are not on record, so every PTX3 structure used the pooled per-copy effect, over the same 2,000 posterior draws as the main analysis. We also traced NRI against the per-copy effect from a log odds ratio of 0 to 1.6.

### Effect modification by neutropenia

Neutropenic admissions were 28% of the cohort (1,157), with an event rate of 9.2% and 106 of the 133 events (80%). ### Genetic architectures that would reach the MCID

Under the profile-preserving truth, of the 4,000 architectures 40% reached the 5% MCID and 77% reached the 20% MCID (@fig-aim4). The pattern was:

- **One locus:** never enough. At an odds ratio of 3.0 and frequency 0.5 it reached 37% of the MCID.
- **Odds ratio 1.5 or less:** no number of independent loci up to 20 reached it at 5% (linked causal loci can; see below).
- **Odds ratio 2.0:** 9 to 12 loci were needed at frequencies of 0.2 to 0.5, and 19 at 0.1.
- **Odds ratio 3.0:** 4 to 5 loci were enough at frequencies of 0.15 to 0.5.

The gain peaks at a frequency of about 0.45 and is slightly lower at 0.5, so the frontier is not strictly monotone in frequency near 0.5 (under the global-intercept truth, for example, an odds ratio of 2.9 needs 3 loci at frequencies of 0.4 and 0.45 and 4 at 0.5).

The global-intercept truth needed 7 to 9 loci at odds ratio 2.0 and 3 to 4 at odds ratio 3.0, so the 5% frontier moved out by two to three loci at an odds ratio of 2.0, and by two at 1.8 (frequency 0.3; @tbl-calibration). Under that truth one architecture with an odds ratio of 1.5 (20 loci at frequency 0.45) reached the 5% MCID, by +0.000007. The profile-preserving truth removes it.

The four-term panel corresponds to about 7 loci of odds ratio 1.3 at frequency 0.3, and would need to be roughly eight times stronger to reach the 5% MCID. Architectures near the frontier depended on the unpublished correlation structure (gain over MCID varied by about 15% across ensemble members, computed under the global-intercept truth). Those well inside or outside it did not.

With estimated weights, a score kept a median of 96% of the ceiling gain at a per-locus precision of σ = 0.10. It kept 89% at σ = 0.20 and 58% at σ = 0.40, which is the range of precision of the present pooled estimates (0.18 to 0.38). At those precisions, between 12% and 36% of the architectures that reached the MCID with exact weights no longer did. The loci needed for a given odds ratio rose by one to several. At σ = 0.40 and an odds ratio of 1.8 (frequency 0.3), no number of loci up to 20 reached the 5% MCID under the profile-preserving truth, against 20 under the global-intercept truth.

Unequal frequencies and locus-specific precision changed this by no more than 3 points. The winner's curse mattered only at σ = 0.40 (9% against 16%; the realised gain kept 0.16 of the exact gain against 0.70). At frequency 0.3 and σ = 0.10, an odds ratio of 2.5 needed 6 loci with the causal variants and with a tag at 0.9 and 19 with a tag at 0.7; an odds ratio of 2.0 needed 11 loci, then 14, then none within 20. At 20% the ordering was the same with smaller losses (67% and 47% for the idealised score, 61% and 39% with a tag at 0.9, 45% and 18% with a tag at 0.7). Linkage among the causal loci raised the share (blocks of four with allele correlations of 0.3 and 0.6: 41% and 52% with joint estimates and 35% and 38% with marginal estimates at σ = 0.10) and let odds ratios of 1.4 and 1.5 reach the MCID in some architectures (joint estimates at a correlation of 0.6: 11 and 24 of 60 architectures at σ = 0.10), so the statement that no panel of loci with odds ratios of 1.5 or less reaches the MCID holds for independent loci only. That result holds every locus's conditional effect fixed within a correlated block, which is a generous assumption.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

On the published evidence, adding the four best-evidenced host variants to a validated clinical score for invasive mould disease would change few decisions at the 5% threshold. With the true effects in the score, the panel found about one extra case per 1,000 admissions, against a pre-specified bar of 4.6. Every combination of evidence reading, cohort, baseline equation and correlation structure we examined gave the same conclusion. The most favourable addition the evidence allows, a second CLEC7A parameter, still left the gain at about a quarter of the MCID.

The conclusion does not depend on how the genetic truth was added to the clinical model. The first version of the simulation held only the overall prevalence, which left the clinical score slightly miscalibrated within profiles. That raised the gains at 5% by 5% to 6% and made the architecture frontier two to three loci easier to reach, but none of the gain was a correction of the miscalibration: the best clinical-only rule under that truth did as well as the clinical score to within 0.00027 in net benefit in every draw. We therefore report the profile-preserving truth, under which the clinical score is calibrated by construction and a gain can only come from the information in genotype. That is the comparison that corresponds to the clinical question, which is whether genotype adds to a score that is already well calibrated.

A real gain of this size would also be hard to observe: it would show as no gain, or a loss, in a fifth to a third of cohorts the size of the derivation cohort. Taken together, the panel behaves like about seven loci of odds ratio 1.3, far from the architectures the sweep shows would be needed.

### Comparison with the earlier reclassification result

An earlier, unpublished analysis of the same question reported a net reclassification improvement (NRI) of 22.5% from adding PTX3 variants to the same clinical score. Our results differ from it for two reasons.

**Linkage.** The earlier analysis simulated rs2305619, rs3816527 and rs1840680 as independent multiplicative risks, although in European haplotypes they tag one haplotype class (r² 0.79 to 0.996, D′ = 1). Its homozygote odds ratio of 15.2 for rs1840680 came from that triple counting. It also used the global dbSNP frequency of rs2305619 (0.36) rather than the non-Finnish European one (0.48), which shrinks the homozygote class from 21% to 13% of the cohort. At the same per-copy effect, the independent structure quadrupled the net-benefit gain (+0.00033 against +0.00008; @fig-nri-grid). Treated correctly as one haplotype, PTX3 adds +0.00008 in net benefit at 5%, about 2% of the MCID. It does not reach the MCID at any per-copy odds ratio up to 5.

**The metric.** NRI counts movements across risk categories without weighing the consequences of the decisions they imply. It can be positive for uninformative markers, and it can reward miscalibration [@kerr2014; @pepe2014; @hilden2013]. Our own four-term haplotype panel had a continuous NRI of 34%, above the earlier 22.5%, with a net-benefit gain an eighth of the MCID. An NRI of this size is therefore no evidence that decisions would change.

Genotype does improve the ranking (aROC +0.012), but few admissions cross the threshold. The extra admissions flagged at 5% included about nine false positives for every extra true case (8.9 under the profile-preserving truth). At this threshold a true case is worth up to 19 false positives, so these moves are beneficial on balance, but there are too few of them to matter.

Continuous NRI also failed to register harm. The three-SNP score applied to real, linked chromosomes lowered net benefit yet had a positive continuous NRI (29%), and the measure's value and components swung with tiny shifts in heterozygotes' predicted risk: for the correct h2 term it was 13% under one construction of the truth and 34% under the other, with the same net-benefit gain. Net benefit at a clinically chosen threshold, judged against a difference fixed in advance, asks the question a clinician would ask: does the score find more patients who will develop disease without treating more who will not [@vickers2006]?

### Where genetic information might matter

The analyses point to four settings where genotype could carry more value. Each comes with qualifications.

**At a higher threshold.** At the secondary 20% threshold, fewer admissions are flagged and each extra true case is worth fewer false positives. There the five-term ceiling score reached the MCID in 64% of draws. This result is secondary, rests on a parameter estimated from three studies, and should be read as a hypothesis.

**In neutropenic admissions.** Neutropenic admissions hold 80% of the events. They supplied about four-fifths of the gain whichever way the effect modification ran, unless the effect was placed entirely in non-neutropenic admissions. The only stratified data [@brunel2018] point the opposite way to the inflammatory-levelling hypothesis. They come from one small study with a different definition of neutropenia, so they do not settle the question.

The design analysis shows what a genotyped cohort of this size could do, provided genotype is measured and the weights are right. With the five-term score, it would detect a modification of the size the inflammatory-levelling argument implies in 60% to 89% of studies, and a modification of Brunel's size almost always. Of the follow-up studies these results suggest, this one would be the most informative. It would test the biology directly. It would also show whether a single pooled score is safe to use: under Brunel's pattern, such a score would do harm.

**With larger effects or more loci.** Reaching the frontier needs loci with odds ratios of 2 or more, or many loci with precise weights. A per-locus precision of 0.1 on the log scale needs a discovery sample of about 1,000 balanced subjects per locus at a common frequency. A cohort the size of the Bologna one gives 0.14 to 0.22. To our knowledge no genome-wide association study of invasive aspergillosis has been published. The candidate-gene literature cannot supply the number of precisely estimated loci the sweep shows would be needed. These requirements also assume that every case responds to the loci. If only some of the moulds do, they rise sharply: at 80% the loci needed roughly doubled, and at 60% or less no architecture in the grid reached the 5% MCID. Variants specific to one pathogen are therefore a weak basis for a score that decides for all moulds, and better suited to a pathogen-specific decision. The requirements also assume that the scored variants are the causal ones. A tag correlated 0.7 with the causal variant kept under half of the gain, and then no number of loci up to 20 reached the 5% MCID at an odds ratio of 2.0; a tag correlated 0.9 cost a fifth of the gain. The frontier is a statement about independent, causal, well-measured loci.

**In allogeneic transplant recipients.** Here the biological case for donor genotype is strongest [@cunha2014]. But the baseline already flags 77% of these admissions at 5%, which leaves little room for genotype to add. At 20% donor genotype could plausibly reach the stratum's MCID. The evidence is one or two studies per term, however, and none for CD209.

### Strengths and limitations

The study has six main strengths:

- The MCID, the primary threshold and the panel were fixed before any genetic result existed and locked in version control. Every later change is a dated addendum that discloses what had been seen.
- Gains were computed exactly rather than by Monte Carlo sampling.
- Linked SNPs were modelled as haplotypes.
- Every primary-analysis contrast with an ambiguous reference group was checked against its source, and errors were corrected and logged. The predictions for the neutropenia analysis were committed before it was run.
- The clinical baseline comes from individual admission-level data rather than published margins.
- The code, tests and summary outputs are public.

The limitations fall into seven groups.

**The evidence is thin.** Each term rests on three to five studies. Some cohorts overlap, notably the Spanish registry series and the Jena AML cohorts, and that overlap has only partly been adjudicated. The record of the human round-2 verification is not yet in the repository. The direction of one donor study's recessive PTX3 rows (Fisher 2017) is inferred from an allele-order footnote (addenda 39 and 40). Small candidate-gene studies are prone to publication bias and inflated effects. If so, our pooled effects are optimistic, which would strengthen the negative conclusion at 5% rather than weaken it.

**The mapping rests on assumptions.** Converting heterogeneous contrasts to one per-copy scale assumes additive effects and Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. It also uses European haplotype frequencies for every cohort except the two East Asian studies in the CLEC7A sensitivity analysis. The priors on μ and τ, and the continuity correction for TLR4, are analytic choices with little data to check them against.

**Several simulation assumptions favour the genetic score.** The realised score uses posterior medians, which are already shrunk, and it ignores the winner's curse and the incomplete tagging of causal variants. In stress tests on odds ratios of 2 or more, the winner's curse and unequal precision changed the loci needed little, but a tag correlated 0.7 with the causal variant kept under half of the gain (addendum 42). Real-world realised gains would therefore be lower.

**Other simulation assumptions could cut either way.** Genotype is assumed independent across genes and independent of the clinical profile. The analysis considers one threshold decision per admission. Only candidate genes are modelled.

**The construction of the genetic truth.** The profile-preserving truth adds the same logit-scale effect in every clinical profile and compensates with an intercept per profile. Other forms of interaction between genotype and clinical risk were not examined. The primary truth was adopted after the global-intercept results were known (addendum 24), though it was not tuned to them. Every analysis in this paper, including the sampling-variability, correlation-ensemble, attribution, effect-modification, donor-stratum and robustness analyses, was run under it, and the first version's global intercept was kept as a sensitivity analysis (addenda 24 to 34). Compared with that sensitivity analysis, the global intercept overstated gains at 5% by 5% to 6% in the panels, let 241 of 4,000 architectures reach the MCID that did not under the profile-preserving truth, and made the scenario with all of the effect in non-neutropenic admissions look more favourable (the oracle score reached the 5% MCID in 18% of draws against 2%). It changed little at 20% and did not change the neutropenia pattern, the attribution or the donor result. The non-neutropenic scenario is the one most sensitive to the construction because it puts a per-copy effect about five times the pooled one into a low-risk stratum. There the single intercept flattens the clinical gradient: the lowest-risk profiles, which hold most of the stratum, get a true risk 14% to 29% above the clinical score's, and the highest-risk ones a much lower one. This is a different simulated population, not a correction of the comparator: against the best clinical-only rule the gain under the global intercept was almost the same (3.01 against 3.05, per 1,000 admissions at 5% and the pooled estimates). The gain was 11% lower under the profile-preserving truth, which is enough to move the MCID from the 82nd to above the 95th percentile of the draws (addendum 26). The other scenarios would be distorted too at effects that large, by 11% to 28% in the median gain and more where the large effect is in the neutropenic stratum, but none of them has one: their stratum effects are at most 2.5 times the pooled one, and they differ by 3% to 8% (addendum 27). The five-term panel and the realised score behave in the same way, apart from the realised score in the scenario with all of the effect in non-neutropenic admissions, where its gain is negligible under both constructions (addendum 28). The reason is that the global intercept raises the true risk of the lowest-risk profiles and lowers it in the higher-risk ones: that helps a score strong enough to flag the former, as the ceiling score is, and hurts one that reaches only the latter, as the pooled weights of the realised score do (addenda 30 to 32, which test it by changing the truth band by band, for four and five terms). The distortion exceeds 10% only when the panel's effects are 2.4 to 2.7 times the pooled ones (1.6 to 1.8 times for five terms), or when a single dominant term has a per-copy odds ratio of 3 to 5.5; with every term at the 95th percentile of its posterior it is 9% (four terms) and 12% (five terms) at 5% and 0.5% to 2% at 20% (addendum 29).

**The second CLEC7A parameter is the least mature part of the evidence.** It comes from three studies. The opposite direction in East Asian studies fits a tag SNP whose linkage to a causal variant differs between ancestries, but the causal variant has not been identified.

**The cohort is a single pre-pandemic centre.** Admissions run from 2007 to 2016, and the outcome is all invasive moulds, not aspergillosis alone. The baseline is evaluated on the data it was fitted to, although the optimism was small (0.005 in aROC). Prophylaxis practice has changed since 2016, and the score's calibration today is unknown.

**The decision framework is narrow.** Net benefit at a fixed threshold captures one use of a risk score. The MCID of 20% more true cases is a judgement, though the strictest of the three readings we considered. Other uses of genotype, such as choosing an antifungal agent or enriching a trial, were not evaluated.

The stress tests of locus-specific precision, the winner's curse, incomplete tagging and linkage among the causal loci were run within the pathogen-specificity grid and on the main grid at f = 1, one setting each (blocks of four loci for linkage). The pathogen-specificity scenario rests on an assumed share f of cases due to the target pathogen, because the cohort has no pathogen-level outcome.

The NRI comparison rests on the pooled PTX3 effect, because the earlier analysis's per-SNP inputs are not on record, and we do not know which form of NRI it reported.

### Conclusions

On the published evidence, host genotype at PTX3, CLEC7A, TLR4 and CD209 is unlikely to change decisions at the 5% threshold for invasive mould disease in haematological inpatients. A study the size of the derivation cohort could not reliably detect the gain it would give. Genotyping would become worth doing only with variants of larger effect, or many more of them, than candidate-gene studies have found. The architecture frontier behind that statement assumes independent, causal, well-measured loci that act on all the moulds in the outcome. It moves out when only some of the moulds respond or when the scored variant only tags the causal one (a correlation of 0.7 kept under half of the gain), and it moves in only if distinct causal loci are linked, so its numbers are conditional on those assumptions. A more immediate use for a genotyped cohort of this size would be to test whether genetic effects depend on neutropenia, which would also show whether a single pooled score is safe.
